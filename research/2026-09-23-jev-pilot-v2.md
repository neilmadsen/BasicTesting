# Jev pilot v2: most decisions, escalation to the strategist

Follow-up to [the first spike](2026-09-23-jev-pilot.md). v1 let Jev choose only what to
cast in our own main phase. Its main lesson was that the pilot lacked control
of the decisions that decide games. v2 gives Jev nearly every decision Forge's
AI makes for our seat. It also lets Jev call the strategist back when the board
changes under the plan.

**Short answer:**
- The machinery works, is cheap, and plays the deck more fully.
- When the pilot departs from Forge's choice, a blind judge prefers the pilot's
  choice about 2 to 1 (72 vs 38, p = 0.0015, with the strategy memo hidden
  from the judge).
- It does not yet win more games. The best arm won 4/24 against Forge's 5/24.
  All five pilot arms together won 11/120 against 5/24 (p = 0.15).

Most of what the experiments found were *framing bugs*: places where the
wording of a choice, or how often it was offered, pushed Jev toward bad play.
Win rate rose with each round of fixes (2 → 3 → 4 of 24). Each step is within
noise, and the fixes were found on these same pods, so treat that trend as a
hint, not a result.

## What v2 does

The Java controller (`pilot/src/edh/pilot`) routes each decision point through a
single multi-question request. Every question carries Forge's own answer as the
default. Jev answers all of them in one call and overrules a default only when
its choice beats it by a probability margin. Every hook falls back to Forge's
answer on any error. A loop breaker defers to Forge when a decision repeats
more than 8 times in one phase.

| kind | decision |
|---|---|
| action | priority at any speed: main phase, responses to an opponent's spell, opponent's end step, whenever Forge wants to act. Options: Forge's pick, plays Forge approves, lands, plays Forge *declines*, pass |
| tgt_* | the target for every targeted option, asked speculatively in the same call |
| attack / block | per creature: hold or whom to attack. Per attacker: which blocker, if any |
| mulligan, confirm, optional-trigger, choose | keep/mull, "you may" prompts, optional triggers, single-entity effect choices |
| search | tutors, fetch lands, ramp, recursion: one option per distinct name, with type line and text |
| discard, sacrifice, sacrifice-cost | which card goes |
| scry, surveil, trigger-target | per-card top/away; targets for our triggers (ETB removal and the like) |

State includes oracle text for every non-land card in view. Target options carry
P/T, damage, loyalty and controller.

**Escalation.** The sidecar diffs the board against the snapshot the current
memo was written on. It reports lost games, life swings of 8 or more, wipes,
creature swings, our named permanents that left, and our commander leaving.
Permanents we used up on purpose (cracked fetches, sacrifice costs) don't count.
Each Jev call then also answers a yes/no question: "does this make the memo
wrong?". A confident yes (≥ 0.6) has Opus 5.5 re-plan mid-turn, and the decision
is re-asked under the new memo. There is at most one escalation per turn and 8
per game. Memos now end with a REPLAN IF line that names the events to watch for.

## Framing bugs the experiments exposed

Each of these was found by reading the decision logs, not the win rates:

1. **Basics over triomes.** Search options showed "Zagoth Triome (land)", so to
   "find a Swamp or Forest" the basic looked like the literal answer. Jev took
   basics over duals and triomes 12 times out of 15. With type lines in the
   option text, Forge's pick was overruled in 4 of 15, and those swaps were
   between duals.
2. **Phantom losses.** The board diff compared display strings, so tapping Sol
   Ring or ticking a counter on Ratchet Bomb looked like losing it. Jev
   escalated on those at p 0.6–0.8.
3. **Death by "not now".** v2 answered *pass* over Forge's intended play 32% of
   the time, including 156 uncracked fetch lands. Every window's "not now" was
   locally plausible. But Forge re-offers the play at every window, so "later"
   never came. The memo made it worse: Jev alone vetoed 17%, and with memos the
   rate was 32%, because Jev read the memo's HOLD line as a general instruction
   to wait. It did this even when the same memo said "crack Bloodstained Mire".
   Fixes:
   - vetoing Forge's play needs a 0.35 margin instead of 0.15;
   - the pass option names the play it skips;
   - free plays are marked `[costs no mana]`;
   - the strategist is told that HOLD names specific cards, never free plays.

   With those fixes the veto rate fell to 19%, and fetch-crack vetoes fell from
   156 to 6.
4. **A fail-to-find loop.** Survival of the Fittest: Jev chose "take nothing",
   Forge asked for confirmation, Jev said no, and Forge reopened the search. That
   happened 162 times in one game. Searches now offer "take nothing" only when
   Forge would, and the loop breaker catches the general case.
5. **Our own sacrifices escalated.** Cracking our own fetch or activating
   Executioner's Capsule is not news. These are now excluded from the diff.
6. **Stranding the commander.** When Muldrotha died or was exiled, Forge asked
   whether to move it to the command zone. The pilot said no in 5 of 11
   prompts. The audit surfaced this, and it is the kind of rare, catastrophic
   error that an average over decisions hides. That replacement is no longer
   put to the pilot.

The common thread is that **the executor's skill is mostly the option text and
the menu's dynamics**, not its intelligence. Jev chose well among what it was
shown, as it understood it.

## What the logs show about play

- **The pilot plays the whole deck.** Cards never cast: 8 under Forge, 0–3 under
  the pilot, including The One Ring, which Forge's AI never casts. Muldrotha
  arrives a round earlier with Opus memos (round 6.2 against 7.3).
- **It plays more defensively.** In combat it held back 42 creatures Forge would
  have attacked with, and it blocked 10 times where Forge would not. It survives
  longer, but it closes games slower.
- **Without the strategist, the plan barely happens.** Jev alone, with only the
  static deck brief, replayed 1.6–2.0 spells and 0.5–0.9 lands per game from the
  graveyard, against Forge's 4.8 + 1.7. Its commander cast rate was also lower
  (67–75%). The Opus memo is what makes it play *Muldrotha*.
- **Escalation fires on real events.** After the fixes, the triggers were
  opponents' life swings, removal of named pieces (Muldrotha, Grist, Gray
  Merchant), and wipes. About 3.4 escalations per game from 5.5 checks. Each
  re-plan costs about 15 s.

## Cost

- Jev: $0.012–0.021 per game for about 110 decisions and 115 questions per game.
  Latency is 0.4–0.5 s average and under 0.7 s at p95.
- Strategist (Opus 5.5 via lightweight `claude -p`, effort low): about 14 memos
  per game at 15 s each, counting escalations.
- A piloted game with the strategist takes 6–8 minutes, against about 1 minute
  for Forge alone.

## Blind decision audit

`./edh sim ... --pilot jev --log-state` logs the full board, memo and options
for every decision. `./edh pilot-audit` then samples the pilot's overrules and
shows a judge the deck plan, the board and both answers in random order. The
judge is Opus 5.5 at medium effort, and it is not told which answer is
Forge's. The tool tallies which answer the judge prefers.

| sample (v2.2 arm) | pilot better | Forge better | same / unparsed | sign test |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 80 of the first 257 overrules, memo shown | 48 | 23 | 5 / 4 | p = 0.004 |
| same 80, memo hidden | 46 | 24 | 5 / 5 | p = 0.012 |
| 120 of all 673 overrules, memo hidden | 72 | 38 | 7 / 3 | p = 0.0015 |

Hiding the memo barely moves the result. The pilot follows the memo, so if the
judge were only rewarding agreement with the plan, the preference would shrink
when the memo is hidden. It didn't.

By kind, **actions** carry the result (64 to 22 in the full sample). **Attacks
are the weak spot**: 11 pilot against 16 Forge across the three audits. That's
not significant (p = 0.44), but it fits the mechanism. Forge's attack AI
simulates combat against every potential blocker, while Jev judges each
creature in isolation from a text board. The judge's reasons for preferring
Forge are mostly combat math, for example "P1 has seven ground blockers, so
Lamia gets blocked".

Caveats:
- The judge is the same model family as the strategist.
- It judges single decisions, not their knock-on effects.
- The audit covers only overrules (31% of the decisions Jev answered). In another
  61% Jev chose the same answer as Forge. In 8% it leaned elsewhere, but not by
  the required margin, so Forge's answer stood.

## Conclusions

1. **As an engineering question, v2 is done.** Jev makes nearly every decision
   for our seat. It costs about $0.02 per game and adds about 0.45 s per
   decision. Every hook falls back to Forge on error, and loops are broken.
   Escalation fires on real events (removal of named pieces, wipes, life
   swings) about 3 times a game, and Opus re-plans in about 15 s. Every
   decision and memo is logged.
2. **Decision by decision, the pilot plays better than Forge's AI.** It plays
   Muldrotha's plan: all of the deck gets cast, including cards Forge's AI
   never casts, and the commander arrives earlier. That makes it useful now
   for the question "does this deck work when played as designed?" The memos
   are also a readable how-to-pilot narrative.
3. **It is not yet a better *winner*.** No arm beat Forge on win rate.
   Plausible reasons:
   - Rare catastrophic errors dominate outcomes and are invisible in averages.
     The commander-zone bug is an example.
   - Mixed control. Some decisions never reach Jev, and Forge makes them without
     knowing the plan: which lands to tap, X values, modes of modal spells, the
     order of simultaneous triggers, multi-target and multi-card choices, and the
     choices Forge's AI makes inside individual card effects. Forge also builds
     every menu Jev chooses from.
   - Attacks are weaker than Forge's.
   - Two of the six pods are unwinnable for this deck under any pilot.
4. **For deck work, keep Forge-only sims as the A/B baseline.** They are 6–8×
   faster and their bias is at least consistent. Use `--pilot jev --strategist
   claude-cli` to see a deck played as intended, and `pilot-audit` to check the
   pilot itself. Don't quote piloted win rates as a deck's strength yet.

**Next steps, most useful first:**
- **Put Forge's combat prediction into attack options.** Forge can predict for
  each attacker whether it would be blocked, killed or unblocked. Adding that
  as option text fixes the weakest decision kind with a framing change, the
  kind of change that has worked every time so far.
- **Keep running the audit loop.** Audit, read the Forge-better cases, find the
  error class, fix the framing, repeat. It found the commander bug with 120
  judge calls, about 5 minutes and no games.
- **A larger, fresh-seed test once the loop runs dry.** About 96 games per arm
  on new pods would separate a 10-point difference. That takes about 4 hours
  and about 1,300 Opus memos.
