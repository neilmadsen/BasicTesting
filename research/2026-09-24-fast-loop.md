# A faster loop for executor work

> **Correction (2026-09-25):** the static-plan result below (2.42) was a lucky 24-game sample. Over 48 games, twice, Jev with a static plan places about 0.4 worse than Forge's AI, while the Opus strategist with a threat order brings the pilot level with Forge. See [the threat-order note](2026-09-25-threat-order.md). The static loop is still the cheap test bed, but it is not a neutral one.

Follow-up to [the post-mortem](2026-09-24-postmortem.md). Its main finding was that the pilot loses on
execution, not strategy. So the aim here was a loop that tests executor changes in minutes rather than
hours, and a check of whether a sparser strategist is good enough to support it.

## Tools

- **`--strategist-every K`** plans at every K-th of our turns, and on escalation. Each memo gains a NEXT
  TURNS line of conditional plays for the turns until the next plan. Jev is told the memo's age, and
  plan tags follow NEXT TURNS once THIS TURN is over.
- **Escalation limits.** Escalations are capped at one per round and written as quick re-plans (low
  effort, no verify pass). Once the memo is older than this turn, they are measured against the board
  at the start of the round, not the board the memo was written on.
- **`./edh scorecard`** reports per-game counts for each mistake class the post-mortem found, read from
  the logs with no model calls. From the pod logs, so Forge-only runs too, it adds:
  - **finishing place**: 1 = won, 4 = first out;
  - **pressure**: attackers sent, combat damage dealt, life the opponents lost, damage taken.
- **`./edh replay`** re-asks every logged decision of a run under the current executor: about 25 s for
  400 decisions, Jev calls only. It reports what changes, by decision kind, and can blind-judge a
  sample. Use it for Python-side changes; Java-side changes need games.
- **Opponent triggers.** Jev and the strategist both get a list of opponents' permanents and emblems
  whose triggers our plays feed (Blood Artist, Grave Pact, Sephiroth's emblem, Rhystic Study).

## Runs

All runs used the same 24 games: seed 11 and the default five pods of 4–5 games. Pods are set by
`--games`, `--seed` and `--per-pod`.

Notes on the table:
- Forge's row counts 6 first places although the sim summary counts 5 wins. One game hit the clock
  after the log recorded a win.
- The standard error of an arm's average place is 0.20–0.24 (standard deviation about 1.0–1.2 over
  24 games). For the difference between two arms it is about 0.3. Only iteration 1's gap to Forge (0.42)
  approaches 1.4 standard errors, and nothing here is significant. Iteration 2, the static arm and
  Forge are indistinguishable.

### Iteration 1: good decisions, passive games

By the blind audits, iteration 1's decisions looked good:
- action overrules: the pilot's pick was preferred 44–13 (p < 0.001);
- blocks, costs, shock payments and targets together: 48–23 (p = 0.004);
- blocks alone: 15–2;
- attacks: 20–24, a coin flip.

Yet it finished last in 10 of 24 games. The pressure lines showed why: it sent 1.9 attackers a game to
Forge's 5.1 and dealt 7 combat damage a game to Forge's 17. Jev held back 1.5 of the 3.2 attacks per
game that Forge proposed.

Each hold looks defensible on its own, which is why the per-decision audit rated them a coin flip.
Added up, opponents were left to develop unchecked. This is the pass-veto trap again, in combat.

**Per-decision audits can't see tempo. Read the pressure lines before believing an audit.**

Two other problems surfaced in the same run:
- Escalations outnumbered scheduled plans (119 to 51). The check diffed against the board a three-turn
  memo was written on, so ordinary development looked like a shock.
- Memo latency: 47 s on average, and 110–230 s late in the game.

### Iteration 2: pressure back, placement at Forge's level

Changes:
- Holding back an attacker Forge sends now needs the 0.35 margin. Replaying iteration 1's log, 22 of
  209 attack answers went back to Forge's attack.
- Escalations use the round-start baseline.

Results:
- Attackers sent rose from 1.9 to 3.1 a game.
- Last places fell from 10 to 6, and average place went from 3.00 to 2.67.
- Escalations halved (2.7 a game).

Forge now proposes 3.6 attacks a game on the pilot's boards, and we make 3.2 of them. The remaining gap
to Forge-alone's 5.1 is upstream: the pilot's boards have fewer attack-ready creatures.

### Jev with a static plan

The same executor with no strategist at all (the deck's brief and notes as the plan) placed 2.42. That
is the best of the four, but within noise of iteration 2 and of Forge. It also:
- dies earlier (round 9.7) and takes more damage;
- finishes second far more often (10 of 24);
- takes 12.5 minutes for 24 games, and costs about $0.40 of Jev and no Claude usage.

## What this says

- **The strategist has no measurable edge at this sample size.** Planning every third turn, the Opus
  strategist isn't distinguishable from a static plan: 2.67 against 2.42, a difference of under one
  standard error. It costs 4× the wall-clock and about 130 Opus calls per 24 games.
  - This doesn't show the strategist is useless. The memo audits and the post-mortem found its plans
    mostly right.
  - It does show that execution, not planning, limits results now.
- **The loop for executor work:**
  1. Change the executor. For Python-side changes, check with `./edh replay` first (seconds to
     minutes).
  2. Run a Jev + static-plan arm on a fixed pod set, about 12 minutes.
  3. Compare with `./edh scorecard` against the same pods' Forge baseline, which is run once per pod
     set. Look at finishing place and pressure first, then at the mistake-class counts.
  4. Use blind audits and strategist arms occasionally, not every iteration.
- **Sample size.** At 24 games per arm, only a difference of about 0.6 in average place between two
  arms is significant at 95%. To resolve 0.3, run about 96 games per arm, e.g. four pod sets. With the
  Jev-only arm that is under an hour, and the Forge baseline is run once per pod set.

## Next executor targets

- **Deployment.** On the pilot's boards Forge proposes 3.6 attacks a game, against 5.1 when Forge plays
  the whole game. Jev's action choices develop fewer attack-ready creatures: removal and value engines
  come ahead of bodies.
- **Attack targets.** Jev's retargeting was 2–0 in earlier audits. Still unmeasured: whether it spreads
  damage or focuses the player closest to winning.
- **Trigger targets.** Still a coin flip (9–9).
